Case 01 — Audiovisual Semantic Annotation

From audiovisual content to structured semantic labels

Context: An audiovisual annotation project for a Belgium-based localization agency. The agency and end client are not named to respect confidentiality.

My contribution: I manually interpreted video content and assigned labels to distinct semantic elements. This was not limited to transcription: it required identifying who or what was represented, what was happening, and what communicative or emotional information was conveyed.

ActorCharacterActionIntentionSpeechEmotion / sentiment

Annotation dimensions

Actor: the visible or audible performer / source.

Character: the represented role or identity in the scene.

Action: the observable event or behavior.

Intention: the communicative purpose inferred from contextual evidence.

Speech: spoken content and its relation to the scene.

Emotion: the affective tone conveyed in context.

Quality considerations

Labels must reflect the audiovisual evidence rather than assumptions based on isolated words or gestures.

Distinguishing observable action from inferred intention is important for consistent annotation.

Ambiguous cases require careful interpretation and adherence to the project's labeling criteria.

Why this matters for AI data quality: the task turns unstructured audiovisual material into structured, semantically meaningful labels. It requires contextual judgment, consistent categorization, and attention to the boundaries between observation and interpretation.

Case 02 — AI-Generated Subtitle Evaluation & Error Taxonomy

Making model feedback specific, consistent, and actionable

Context: A separate AI-generated subtitle evaluation project. I reviewed model outputs, identified errors, assessed their severity, and documented findings to support quality improvement.

18error categories
with severity levels in the feedback framework adopted by the team
EXAMPLE 01

Repetition hallucination

Repeated words or phrases in generated subtitles without corresponding evidence in the audio.

EXAMPLE 02

Thematic-role violation

Agent and patient roles are exchanged, changing who performs or receives an action.

EXAMPLE 03

Logical-order breakdown

Events or instructions appear in an incoherent causal or sequential order.

These examples come from a separate analytical exercise on AI subtitle outputs. They illustrate the category-card presentation and are not the full 18-category taxonomy.

Initial problem

The feedback sheet provided a single generic field. This made it difficult to distinguish error types, compare recurring patterns, or communicate the nature and impact of each issue consistently.

Proposed improvement

I proposed an error taxonomy with 18 categories and severity levels. The team adopted the structure, making review feedback more specific and easier to interpret.

How the evaluation worked

  1. Review the AI-generated subtitle against the audiovisual source and task requirements.
  2. Identify the issue and distinguish it from acceptable language variation.
  3. Assign the relevant error category.
  4. Assess severity according to the impact on meaning, comprehension, or subtitle quality.
  5. Record concise, actionable feedback.

Illustrative feedback structure

FieldPurpose
Error categoryClassifies the type of issue using the team's taxonomy.
SeverityIndicates the impact of the issue.
Evidence / contextPoints to the relevant subtitle or audiovisual moment.
Reviewer commentExplains why the output needs attention.

This is a presentation of the framework's fields, not a reproduction of the original confidential form or the full 18-category taxonomy.

Outcome: the proposed 18-category taxonomy and severity levels were adopted by the team. The improvement made feedback more structured and useful for identifying recurring model issues.

Case 06 — Python Annotation Template

Standardizing the structure before automating the task

This Python exercise establishes a predefined structure and fields for a subtitle-related annotation workflow. Its purpose is standardization, not automatic annotation.

What the script does

  • Sets up a consistent template.
  • Defines the expected fields and their order.
  • Provides a repeatable structure for manual data entry.

What it does not do

  • It does not perform NER.
  • It does not identify speakers or voices automatically.
  • It does not infer actors, intentions, or emotions.
  • It does not populate annotation fields autonomously.
